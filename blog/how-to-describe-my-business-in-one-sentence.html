How to describe my business in one sentence

23 November 2026 · Nunya Bunya

Why your answer changes every time

Someone at a barbecue asks what you do. You say "I run a small mobile mechanic business." A week later, at a networking event, it comes out as "I do car servicing and repairs, kind of anywhere." On the phone to a possible customer it becomes "we do all sorts." None of these is wrong, but none of them tells the listener why they should call you instead of the garage ten minutes from their house, which is probably cheaper and has a sign out the front.

The fix is a single sentence you decide on once and then use everywhere. That sentence has a name, a positioning statement, and this article walks you through writing one.

What a positioning statement is

A positioning statement is one sentence that says who you help, what problem you solve for them, and why they should pick you over their other options. It is a working tool for you and your customers. You can set it up without a designer, a consultant or a budget.

The method

You need a pen and about 20 minutes of quiet for the first draft. You will answer four questions, then join the answers into a sentence.

Step 1: Pick the person

Write down the type of customer you most want more of. Describe a situation, not a demographic. "Everyone with a car" is a population. "People who work weekdays and can't spare a morning at a garage" is someone you could picture and find. If you have a few customer types, pick the one who is easiest to serve and most pleasant to deal with, then add the others later.

Step 2: Name the problem in their words

Think back to what customers actually said when they first rang or messaged. Look at your enquiry emails, reviews and texts if you have them. People rarely say "I need a preventative maintenance service." They say "I can't be without the car all day" or "I got burnt by a quote that doubled." Use their phrasing, because it is the phrasing other people like them will recognise.

Step 3: Name what they would do otherwise

This is the step most people skip. Your real competitor is whatever the customer would do if you did not exist. It might be the cheaper operator down the road, doing it themselves, putting it off, or asking a mate. Write down that alternative and what is annoying about it. The annoying part is where your reason to exist sits.

Step 4: Name what you do differently, in a way that can be checked

Write down what you do that the alternative does not. It has to be something a stranger could verify or at least picture. "High quality service" cannot be checked. "A written quote before any work starts" can. "I come to you" can. "Old parts handed back so you can see what was replaced" can.

Step 5: Join it up

Use this frame and adjust the wording until it sounds like you talking:

I help [the person] who [their situation or problem] by [what you do differently], so they get [what they care about].

A worked example

This is an imaginary business, made up for this article. It is not a real client. Say there is a mobile mechanic in Brisbane called Sam.

  1. The person: Parents and shift workers who cannot easily give up a weekday morning.
  2. The problem in their words: "I can't be without the car for a whole day" and "I never know what the final bill will be."
  3. What they do otherwise: Book into the local garage, which is cheaper per hour, then arrange lifts, wait around and get a bill that differs from the phone quote.
  4. What Sam does differently: Works at the customer's home or workplace, gives a written quote before starting, and hands back the old parts.

First draft of the sentence: "I help busy parents and shift workers keep their cars running by servicing and repairing them at home or work, with a written quote before I start, so there is no lost day and no surprise bill."

That is a decent start, but a bit long to say aloud. Trimmed for speaking: "I fix cars at your house or workplace, so you don't lose a day, and you get a written quote before I touch anything."

Notice that Sam's sentence does not mention price at all. It gives a different reason to choose, and for the right customer a different reason is enough to look past the cheaper hourly rate.

"Professional dog walking services tailored to your needs.""I walk dogs for people who work long days, in groups of no more than four, and send a photo from every walk."

(The bookkeeper and dog walker lines are also invented examples.)

Two checks tell you quickly whether your sentence is any good. The first is the swap test: put your nearest competitor's name in place of yours. If the sentence still fits them just as well, it is too vague. The second is the stranger test: read it to someone outside your industry and ask what they would expect to happen if they hired you. If they can tell you something specific and correct, the sentence is working.

The three mistakes that make the exercise useless

1. Describing your services instead of your customer

A list such as "servicing, brakes, roadworthies, diagnostics" tells people what is on the menu. It does not tell them whether the menu is for them. Start with the person and their situation, and let the services fall in behind.

2. Using words anyone could claim

Quality, reliable, friendly, professional, tailored, passionate and trusted are words every competitor also uses, so they carry no information. If a word would survive being printed on any rival's van, cut it and replace it with something observable: a thing you do, a thing you hand over, a time you turn up.

3. Trying to fit everybody in

Naming one type of customer feels like turning the rest away. In practice, the sentence is only a description of where you are strongest, and you can still serve anyone who asks. A sentence broad enough to cover every possible customer ends up describing no one, and you are back to the answer that changes every time.

What to do once it is written

Most articles stop at the sentence. The sentence is only useful once it is in the places where people meet your business, so here is a sequence that works for a one-person operation.

  1. Say it out loud to five real people this week. Friends, existing customers, someone in a different trade. Ask each what they think you do and who it is for. If their answer is far from yours, adjust the wording, not the people.
  2. Make two versions. One short enough to say in a breath, as in the example above. One longer, with the proof points, for your website and quotes.
  3. Put it on the first thing people read. That means the top line of your website homepage, your Google Business Profile description, the bio on each social account and your email signature.
  4. Build it into your quote or proposal template. A short line near the top saying who you do this for and how you work reminds the customer why they enquired, which helps when they are comparing quotes.
  5. Use it as a filter for decisions. When a new service, ad or post idea comes up, ask whether it serves the person in the sentence. If it does not, it can wait.
  6. Ask each new customer why they chose you. Write their answers down word for word. After a dozen or so, compare them with your sentence. Where customers use different words from yours, take their words and put them in the sentence.
  7. Review it a couple of times a year. Businesses drift. If your best customers have changed, or you have added something that matters, update the sentence and then update the places it appears.

A sentence that sits in a notebook changes nothing. Used consistently, it makes the way you describe your business the same each time, so the people who hear it have something to remember and repeat.

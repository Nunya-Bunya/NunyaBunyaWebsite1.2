How to handle price objections

02 November 2026 · Nunya Bunya

Why some enquiries become work and others quietly disappear

You send a quote. Sometimes the reply is "Great, when can you start?" Sometimes it is "That's a bit more than I was expecting," and then nothing. You can't say what separated the two. Each conversation is improvised, so each one goes a little differently, and when it goes badly you never learn why.

A price objection is any moment a prospect signals that your price is a problem. It might be "that's too expensive," "I've seen it cheaper," "I'll need to think about it," or just silence after the quote. The usual reaction is to offer a discount on the spot. That teaches the customer your first price was negotiable, and it cuts your own pay for the same work.

A better approach is to work out in advance what is really going on behind the objection, and to have honest words ready for each case. You can do that with a pen and an hour of thinking. Here is the method.

The method, step by step

Step 1: Collect the real objections

Open your sent emails, texts and notes. Find the last five to ten times someone hesitated over price. Write down their exact words, then what happened next: did they book, go quiet, or say no? If you have fewer than five, write down the ones you dread hearing. Real phrasing matters, because the script you write later has to answer what people actually say.

Step 2: Sort them by what they really mean

"Too expensive" is usually a cover for one of a handful of different problems. Sort each objection into the closest of these:

  • They don't see what is included. They are comparing your price with something that isn't the same job.
  • They don't see the value. They understand the job but can't tell what it saves or protects them from.
  • The timing is the problem. They could afford it, just not this week or all at once.
  • They need someone else to agree. A partner, a boss or a landlord has to approve.
  • They are a poor fit. Their budget is well below what the work costs, and no wording will change that.

Each type needs a different reply. A discount only suits the last one, and even then you are usually better off saying no politely.

Step 3: Write one question for each type

Before you defend your price, find out which type you are dealing with. The best single question is a calm "Is it the total, or how it compares with something you've seen?" Both answers tell you what to do next. If they name a cheaper quote, ask what that quote includes. If they say it's the total, ask whether the timing or the amount is the sticking point.

Step 4: Write a reply that changes the offer, not the rate

If you must give something, adjust the scope (the amount of work covered) or the payment arrangement. Never adjust the hourly rate or the headline price without also adjusting what they get. Smaller scope for a smaller price is fair. The same scope for less money is a discount.

Step 5: Set your walk-away line

Decide now, while you are calm, the lowest job you will take and what you will say when a prospect is below it. A polite, clear "I'm probably not the right fit for that budget, but here's what I'd suggest" is a complete reply.

Step 6: Write the actual words

Write full sentences you could say aloud. A list of bullet reminders will fall apart under pressure.

A worked example: a mobile mechanic

This is an invented example to show the method. It isn't a real client, and the figures are made up.

Suppose a mobile mechanic, someone who drives to the customer and repairs the car in their driveway, quotes $480 to replace the front brake pads and rotors. The customer replies, "That's a lot. The workshop down the road quoted $390."

Going through the steps, the mechanic looks at past hesitations and sees most fit two types: comparison with another quote, and customers who needed to check with a partner. This one is a comparison.

The mechanic's reply, written out in full:

"Fair enough, it's worth comparing. Do you know what their quote includes? Mine covers the parts, the labour, a test drive afterwards, and I come to you, so you don't lose half a day dropping the car off. If theirs covers the same things, I'd understand going with them. If you'd like, send me their quote and I'll tell you honestly where the two differ."

That reply does four things. It agrees that comparing is sensible. It asks a question. It names what the price covers in terms the customer cares about (their time). It offers to help them check, which signals confidence without pushing.

If the other quote turns out to cover cheaper parts, the mechanic can offer a lower-cost parts option at a lower price, which changes the scope. They don't just knock $90 off the same job.

If the customer says they need to ask their partner, the mechanic's written reply is different: "Of course. Would it help if I put the breakdown in an email you can forward?" Same method, different type, different words.

What good looks like compared with what most people write

Most people produce a single paragraph that defends the price: "Our rates reflect our experience, quality and the overheads of running a business." It reads like a policy and sounds like an argument, and the customer hears a defensive person.

A good version has these qualities:

  • It is sorted by objection type, so you pick the right reply instead of one stock answer.
  • Every reply starts with a question or an acknowledgement, never a defence.
  • It describes what the customer gets in their own terms, such as time saved, risk avoided or hassle removed, rather than your costs.
  • Any concession changes the scope or the payment arrangement.
  • It includes a clear, courteous way to part ways when the fit is wrong.
  • It sounds like you. If you wouldn't say a sentence out loud, cross it out.

The three mistakes that make the exercise useless

1. Writing replies to imagined objections

If you skip step 1 and invent objections from your head, you will script replies to things nobody says. Use real messages, even if you only have a few. The real wording is where you find out, for example, that half your hesitations are about timing rather than the amount.

2. Treating every objection as a request for a discount

If every reply ends in a lower price, you have written a negotiation script, not a diagnosis. The sorting step exists to stop that. Many objections can be resolved with information, a smaller scope, or a payment plan.

3. Never deciding where you walk away

Without a floor, every conversation drifts toward the cheapest outcome. The walk-away line is the part people skip because it feels uncomfortable, and it is the part that protects you most. Write it down before the next awkward phone call, not during it.

What to do with it once it is written

Most people write a script like this, feel good about it and never open it again. A few habits keep it useful.

  • Put it where you work. Keep it in the notes app on your phone or as a pinned document, so you can read it before a call or while drafting a reply.
  • Turn the written replies into saved text snippets. Most email and messaging apps let you store short replies you can drop in and edit. Use them as a starting point, then adjust the first line for the person.
  • Log every objection from now on. After each enquiry, note the customer's words, which type you judged it to be, what you said and what happened. A simple spreadsheet with four columns is enough.
  • Review it monthly. Read the log and look for patterns. If most objections are about timing, perhaps you need a payment option. If most are about comparison, perhaps your quote doesn't show clearly what is included. Edit the script to match.
  • Fix the quote itself. Many price objections begin with a quote that is a single number. Listing what is included, in plain words, answers half the objections before they are raised.
